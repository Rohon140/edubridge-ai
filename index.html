Porter's Five Forces: Competitive Intensity Analysis

Core Definition & Purpose

Formulated by Harvard Business School Professor Michael E. Porter in 1979, the Five Forces framework evaluates the competitive environment and long-term profitability of an industry. Rather than viewing competition as merely rival companies, Porter identifies five distinct structural forces shaping market attractiveness.

1. Threat of New Entrants

How easily new competitors enter. Governed by economies of scale, capital requirements, and patents.

2. Supplier Bargaining Power

Suppliers hold power if they are concentrated, have high switching costs, or offer unique inputs (e.g. TSMC in microchips).

3. Buyer Bargaining Power

Consumers pressure prices downward when substitution costs are zero or when purchases represent large volume.

4. Threat of Substitutes

Alternative products fulfilling the same functional need (e.g., High-speed trains substituting domestic flights).

5. Industry Rivalry

The intensity of competition between incumbents. High when competitors are equally sized and industry growth is stagnant.

💡 Real-World BBA Case Example: Commercial Airline Industry

Why are airlines historically low-margin? High Supplier Power: Boeing and Airbus form a duopoly; jet fuel prices are uncontrollable. High Buyer Power: Consumers compare ticket aggregators within seconds with zero switching cost. High Rivalry: Fixed seat inventory perishes the moment the flight takes off.

Strategic Management — Porter's Five Forces & Case Analysis

Generated from Chapter 4 PDF via Gemini AI • Last updated today

1. Overview of Competitive Strategy

The objective of strategic management is to achieve sustainable competitive advantage. Industry structure, rather than temporary operational efficiency, is the primary determinant of long-term return on invested capital (ROIC).

Core Strategic Principle:

"Strategy is not about being the best; it is about being unique. Competing to be the best leads to destructive zero-sum price wars."

2. Critical Formula for Market Valuation (DCF Recap)

Enterprise Value (EV) = ∑ [ Free Cash Flow_t / (1 + WACC)^t ] + Terminal Value

Where WACC is Weighted Average Cost of Capital, reflecting the required rate of return for debt and equity holders.

3. Recommended Case Attack Framework

  1. Identify the Core Dilemma: Differentiate between surface symptoms (declining sales) and structural causes (entrant disruption).
  2. Apply Porter's 5 Forces: Gauge which specific force is compressing profit margins.
  3. Formulate Strategic Choices: Cost Leadership vs. Differentiation vs. Focus Niche.
  4. Mitigate Implementation Risks: Financial feasibility and stakeholder alignment.
